# Resistance Training Program Design

## What it is and why coaches use it

Program design turns a client's goal, schedule, equipment and training history into a plan: which exercises, in what order, for how many sets and reps, with what load and rest, how often, and how it progresses over weeks. Without a plan, training tends to drift: the same sessions repeated with no progression, or random variety with no overload.

A program starts with a needs analysis: what the goal demands (strength, muscle size, power, muscular endurance, sport performance), what the person brings (training age, injury history, available days and time, equipment), and any limits that must be respected.

## Key principles

- **Specificity.** Adaptations match the training: heavy loads for maximal strength, fast movements for power, more total work for muscle size.
- **Progressive overload.** The stimulus must increase over time (more load, reps, sets, or harder variations) or progress stalls.
- **Training age matters more than age.** A beginner progresses from almost any sensible program; trained lifters need more volume, more specific loading and planned variation.
- **Multi-joint before single-joint.** Large, technical, multi-joint lifts go early in the session when the person is fresh; single-joint and core work go later.
- **Recovery is part of the plan.** Hard sessions for the same muscles are usually separated by about 48 hours.
- **Individualization.** Start below what the person can do, and adjust from how they respond.

## Programming guidelines

- **Load and reps by goal (common starting points):**
  - Maximal strength: heavy loads, roughly 1–6 reps per set, long rests (about 2–5 minutes).
  - Muscle size (hypertrophy): moderate loads, roughly 6–12 reps per set, though a wide range of loads works when sets are taken close to failure; rests of about 1–3 minutes.
  - Muscular endurance: light loads, about 15+ reps per set, short rests (about 30–60 seconds).
  - Power: light to moderate loads moved as fast as possible, low reps (about 1–5) with full recovery between sets.
- **Sets:** beginners can start with 1–3 sets per exercise; trained lifters often need more total weekly sets per muscle.
- **Frequency:** train each major muscle group at least 2 days a week; beginners commonly use 2–3 full-body sessions a week.
- **Splits:** full-body (2–3 days), upper/lower (4 days), or body-part splits (4–6 days) for more advanced lifters. Pick the split that fits the days available, not the reverse.
- **Session order:** power or technical lifts first, then multi-joint strength lifts, then single-joint and core work.
- **Progression:** a common rule of thumb is to add load once the person can do about 2 more reps than the target in the last set for two sessions in a row; typical jumps are small (about 2–10% depending on the lift and body region).
- **Periodization:** plan variation over weeks, for example blocks that shift from more volume to more intensity (linear), or alternate heavier and lighter days within a week (undulating). Plan easier weeks (deloads) every few weeks for trained lifters.

## Exercise examples and progressions

- A beginner full-body session might pair one exercise per main pattern: a squat or lunge, a hinge, a push, a pull, and a carry or core exercise.
- Progress exercises from stable and simple to less stable and more complex: goblet squat to split squat to rear-foot-elevated split squat; glute bridge to hip thrust; kettlebell deadlift to Romanian deadlift.
- Keep the main lifts consistent for several weeks so progress can be measured; rotate accessory exercises more freely.

## Safety and readiness considerations

- Teach technique with light loads before adding weight; stop a set when form breaks down.
- Increase only one variable at a time (load, sets, or frequency), and in small steps.
- Watch for signs of too much: performance dropping across sessions, lingering soreness, poor sleep, joint pain. Reduce volume before adding more.
- People with injuries, chronic conditions, or who are pregnant, older, or young need programs adapted to them; see the guidelines and the population-specific notes, and refer to a health professional when unsure.

## Common mistakes or myths the sources address

- **"Only heavy weights build muscle."** Muscle size can increase across a wide range of loads when sets are taken close to failure; heavy loads matter most for maximal strength.
- **"More is always better."** Gains rise with more weekly volume but with diminishing returns, and recovery limits how much a person can do.
- **"Change the program every session."** Constant variety makes overload hard to track; keep core lifts consistent and progress them.
- **"Beginners need advanced programs."** Simple full-body programs work well for beginners.
