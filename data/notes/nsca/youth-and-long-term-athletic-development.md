# Youth Resistance Training and Long-Term Athletic Development

## What it is and why coaches use it

Long-term athletic development (LTAD) means building general athleticism (skill, strength, power, speed, agility, balance, coordination, endurance) over the whole of childhood and adolescence, for every young person, not just the talented [NSCA 2016 LTAD]. Resistance training is a core tool: when well designed and supervised, the NSCA considers it relatively safe and able to improve strength, power, motor skill, injury resilience and exercise habits [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].

Children are roughly up to 11 (girls) or 13 (boys); adolescents are girls 12–18 and boys 14–18 [NSCA 2016 LTAD; NSCA 2009 Youth RT]. Programs of 8–20 weeks typically add roughly 30% strength beyond normal growth, mostly through nervous-system gains before puberty [NSCA 2009 Youth RT]. LTAD targets two opposite problems: too little activity, and too much of one sport [NSCA 2016 LTAD].

## Key principles

- **Plan by maturity, not birthday.** Same-age children can differ widely in biological maturity, and growth is uneven [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Develop skill and strength together, early.** In a meta-analysis of 34 studies, pre- and early-pubertal youth gained about 50% more motor skill from resistance training than adolescents [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Sample several sports** before narrowing down [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Health and enjoyment first;** aim for durable adaptation, not quick wins [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Conditioning prevents injury.** Well-rounded programs may cut injury likelihood by as much as 50%, and inactivity is itself a risk factor [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Children are not miniature adults;** don't hand them adult programs [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].

## Programming guidelines

- **Starting age:** about 6–7 years, once a child follows instructions and has reasonable balance and postural control; before about 5–6 years, emphasize exploratory play and fundamental skills [NSCA 2016 LTAD]. The older statement tied readiness to sport readiness, generally age 7 or 8 [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Warm-up:** 5–10 minutes of dynamic movement; static stretching belongs in the cool-down [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **First sessions:** learn lifts with very light loads, then 1 or 2 sets of 10–15 reps [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Strength:** 1–3 sets of 6–15 reps; later, heavier 6 to 10RM loads on large-muscle lifts. Loads rise from about 50–70% 1RM (novice) toward 70–85% 1RM (advanced) [NSCA 2009 Youth RT]. An experienced adolescent might do 3 sets of 6–8 on multi-joint lifts and 2 sets of 10–12 on single-joint lifts [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Power:** 1–3 sets of 3–6 reps, not to failure; keep sets under 6–8 reps so speed holds [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Progression:** add about 5–10% load as strength improves [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Rest:** about 1 minute for moderate-intensity work; 2–3 minutes for skill- or power-heavy lifting [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Frequency:** 2–3 non-consecutive days per week (48–72 hours apart); once weekly seems too little for strength gains [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Variation:** periodize; plateaus are common after 8–12 weeks. Include active rest, e.g., 1–3 weeks between seasons [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Sport-load red flags:** one sport for more than 8 months a year; more than 16 hours a week; more weekly practice hours than the child's age; organized sport exceeding free play by more than 2:1 [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Competition:** at least 48 hours of rest beforehand and more than 7 hours of sleep nightly; intensive events of 6 hours or more without enough rest raise injury risk [NSCA 2016 LTAD]. Treat rest blocks as fixed parts of the plan.

## Exercise examples and progressions

- **Early childhood:** fundamental locomotor, object-manipulation and stability skills, plus body-weight control such as gymnastics-style play [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Order:** large-muscle and multi-joint lifts before single-joint work; new technical lifts or plyometrics early, while fresh [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Pairing:** back squat and bench press (heavier) with biceps curls and triceps extensions (lighter) [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Weightlifting path:** front squat, then overhead squat, then snatch and clean and jerk; allow lots of teaching time and a brief reset between reps [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Plyometrics:** hops, skips and jumps; hopscotch already counts [NSCA 2009 Youth RT]. Boys before peak height velocity gained most sprint speed from plyometrics; boys past it did better with strength plus plyometrics [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Trunk:** include abdominal and lower-back strengthening [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Growth spurt:** coordination can dip temporarily; re-groove patterns with lighter loads and use short, external cues [NSCA 2016 LTAD].

## Safety and readiness considerations

- **Qualified supervision every session.** Without it, suitable equipment and a safe space, youth should not resistance train [NSCA 2009 Youth RT]. A qualified professional combines pediatric exercise-science knowledge, coaching skill and a recognized credential such as the CSCS [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Teach safety basics:** collars, spotting, equipment storage, handling bars and plates [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Injury sources:** poor technique, excessive loads, unsafe equipment, unsupervised or home use [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **1RM testing** is acceptable after a learning period with close qualified supervision, never unsupervised [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Screening:** not mandatory for apparently healthy children; recommended with signs or symptoms of disease or a known condition, so refer to a health professional [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **Growth spurt:** injury risk, particularly in the lower limb, peaks; track height, limb length and body mass quarterly [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Overtraining warning signs** (watch and refer on): poor appetite, more injuries, constant tiredness, struggling with loads, frequent respiratory infections, heavy or stiff muscles, poor sleep. A 5-item check (fatigue, sleep, soreness, stress, mood) rated 1 to 5 helps [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **Never use exercise as punishment.** Each statement cites a 12-year-old boy who developed exertional rhabdomyolysis after excessive imposed exercise [NSCA 2009 Youth RT; NSCA 2016 LTAD]. Get parental consent and child assent before testing [NSCA 2016 LTAD].

## Common mistakes or myths the sources address

- **"Lifting is too dangerous."** In one school study, resistance training caused 0.7% of injuries; its risk is no greater than many sports [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **"It stunts growth."** No growth-plate injuries in prospective studies, and supervised training doesn't limit final height [NSCA 2009 Youth RT; NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **"Plyometrics require a 1.5× body-weight squat first."** Not supported [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **"Sport alone is enough."** As the 2009 authors put it, "aspiring young athletes cannot play themselves into shape" [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
- **"10,000 hours, so specialize early."** Misread; some experts needed about 5,000 hours. Youth playing 3 or more sports between 11 and 15 more often reached national level at 16 to 18 [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **"Miss the window and it's gone."** Fixed "windows of opportunity" lack longitudinal support [NSCA 2016 LTAD].
- **"Static stretching is the best warm-up."** It can blunt strength and power [NSCA 2009 Youth RT].
