# Resistance Training for Older Adults

## What it is and why coaches use it

This is progressive resistance training adapted for ageing adults. The statement reviewed randomized trials in people aged 50 and older, and it frames its core intensity target for healthy adults older than 60 [NSCA 2019 Older Adults]. The reasoning is simple. Muscle mass starts slipping after about age 30 and falls faster after 60. Strength declines 2–5 times faster than muscle size, and power drops faster still and matters more for everyday tasks [NSCA 2019 Older Adults]. The authors call disuse "a preventable and reversible factor." Only 8.7% of US adults over 75 do muscle-strengthening activity in their leisure time [NSCA 2019 Older Adults].

## Key principles

- **Individualize, periodize, progress.** Start from a needs assessment, progress by tolerance and recovery, and supervise where possible.
- **Intensity drives strength.** Higher loads give bigger strength gains, while moderate loads still build muscle and function.
- **Train power, not just strength.** Moving moderate loads fast carries over better to daily tasks like rising from a chair.
- **Stop short of failure.** Failure adds no extra adaptation for older trainees.
- **Go multicomponent.** Combine resistance work with aerobic, balance, and functional training.

## Programming guidelines

For healthy older adults [NSCA 2019 Older Adults]:
- **Frequency**: 2–3 sessions per week per muscle group, on nonconsecutive days. If time is short, even 1 session a week can help prevent muscle loss.
- **Exercise selection**: 1–2 multijoint exercises per major muscle group. The legs may respond better to 2, for example leg press plus knee extension. Aim for roughly 8–10 exercises in total, with large muscle groups early in the session.
- **Sets**: beginners and frail clients start with 1 set and build to 2–3.
- **Intensity**: work up to 70–85% 1RM across the periodized plan. About 50–70% 1RM still improves muscle and function. Start wherever the client tolerates.
- **Reps**: 6–12 for strength. Beginners use 10–15 at a lighter load. Stopping at about 50–70% of the reps possible in good form is enough.
- **Power**: 40–60% 1RM, lifted as fast as possible in the concentric phase. Don't slow the load at the end of the movement, and don't go to failure. Over 12 weeks, both 1 and 3 sets of power work improved strength and function in older women.
- **Rest and session length**: studies used 1.5–3 minutes between sets. Function-focused guidance mentions about 2 minutes of rest and 30–60 minute sessions.
- **Function-focused loading path**: begin near 55% 1RM and progress toward 80% as tolerated.
- **Concurrent training**: 2–3 times a week. Do strength before endurance work in the same session. Untrained clients improved with as little as 1 strength and 1 endurance session a week.
- **Periodization model**: in men and women around 70, nonperiodized, block, and daily undulating plans produced similar results after 22 weeks.

## Exercise examples and progressions

- **Multijoint staples**: leg press, chair stands, chest press, pulldown or seated row.
- **Equipment path**: beginners, frail clients, and people with functional limits do well on machines, bands, and isometrics. High-functioning clients gain extra benefit from barbells, dumbbells, kettlebells, and medicine balls [NSCA 2019 Older Adults].
- **Functional carryover**: sit-to-stand, weighted box step-ups, loaded chair stands, and daily tasks practised at different speeds or in balance-challenging positions. Free-weight and machine programmes that included squats and split squats improved static balance.
- **Balance**: narrow or single-leg stance, minimal hand support, and controlled weight shifts had the largest fall-prevention effects. Useful drills include line walking, tandem standing, heel-toe walking, stepping practice, and side-to-side weight transfers.
- **Frail clients**: 3 sets of 8–12 reps, starting at 20–30% 1RM and building toward 80%, 2–3 times a week (most studies used 3). Familiarize them first with bodyweight and daily-task drills such as sit-to-stand. Add fast-tempo work at 30–60% 1RM once form is solid. Introduce endurance work after strength and balance improve: begin with 5–10 minutes, build to 15–30, and keep effort at 12–14 on the Borg RPE scale [NSCA 2019 Older Adults].
- **Care-home settings**: seated programmes using bands, soft weights, ankle weights, and medicine balls still produce gains. Full-body functional exercise likely carries over more to daily function.

## Safety and readiness considerations

- A well-designed programme with technique instruction and spotting is safe for healthy older adults. One review of frail very old adults found a single case of shoulder pain across 20 studies with 2,544 participants [NSCA 2019 Older Adults].
- When injuries do occur, they are linked to heavy repetitive loading, poor positioning or technique, and exercise choice. Take extra care with the shoulder, hip, knee, and spine.
- Build intensity from low to moderate before going high, so tolerance can be judged. Watch joint stress and soreness at high loads. Progress only while the client stays free of symptoms such as dizziness or nausea.
- Lock in good form before adding load, speed, or intensity.
- Clients with unstable or uncontrolled health conditions need medical screening and clearance. That decision belongs to a clinician, not the coach. The statement notes that screening may be unnecessary for people who already exercise and have no symptoms.
- For clients with cognitive impairment, simplify exercises, use visual demonstration, and supervise more closely, with clinical guidance as needs increase.

## Common mistakes or myths the source addresses

- **"Lifting is too risky at this age."** Safety and fear are common barriers, but the evidence supports safe training with good programme design, even for frail people and care-home residents.
- **"Keep it light forever."** Light, low-volume work is a starting point. The largest benefits come from progressing to moderate and higher intensities.
- **"Every set to failure."** Not needed, and failure undermines power output.
- **"Strength is all that matters."** Power is more closely tied to daily function. Past a certain strength level, extra strength may add little for daily activities, which is why functional training is included.
- **"Too old to adapt."** Strength gains appear even beyond 85. Lifelong weightlifters aged 85 matched the power of untrained 65-year-olds [NSCA 2019 Older Adults].
- **"Gains disappear when you stop."** After 1 year of training, people remained stronger than controls through 7 years of detraining. Psychological benefits, however, faded when training stopped.
- **"Strength work is pointless for obesity because it doesn't cause weight loss."** Its benefits occur independently of weight loss.
